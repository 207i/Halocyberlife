The Mini Quantum Induction Battery System

What if the electronics we use every day could continuously recover and manage energy, reducing the need to recharge them?

QIBS explores a miniature magnetic induction generator, a precision short-shaft rotor, actuator-controlled engagement, and a three-supercapacitor energy-management system.

From a 13-year-old hand-drawn invention idea to compact devices, modular circuit boards, future applications, and rigorous testing.

01 / The Beginning

Before the simulations, there were drawings.

More than 13 years ago, I began exploring a compact rotating magnetic device as a personal engineering hobby.

I worked with geometry, a protractor, mechanical layouts, and ideas about how magnetic fields could interact with a small rotating assembly.

I was interested in miniaturization: a compact power system that might eventually fit inside ordinary electronics.

The original concept included a very short shaft, skateboard-style bearing, vertical actuator, magnetic induction mechanism, and three-capacitor electrical system.

I never completed experimental validation at that time. Today, I'm reconstructing the design and investigating what it could realistically accomplish.

04 / Electromagnetic Physics

What actually produces electrical power?

Electromagnetic induction occurs when magnetic flux through a conductor changes.

Faraday's law relates induced voltage to the rate of change of magnetic flux:

Vinduced = −N × dΦ/dt

N represents the coil-turn count, and Φ represents magnetic flux.

Rotating permanent magnets near induction coils can generate electrical voltage. Actual output depends on rotor speed, magnetic geometry, coil construction, and electrical load.

Under electrical loading, Lenz's law produces opposing electromagnetic torque. Maintaining rotation requires mechanical input energy.

Engineering integrity

"Quantum" is part of the QIBS project name, not a demonstrated quantum effect. Permanent magnets are not an unlimited energy source. Induction is a conversion process, and continuous electrical output requires sufficient ongoing energy input or depletion of stored energy.

05 / Mechanical Control

The lift-and-disengage concept

OPERATING MODE A

Rotor Engaged

The actuator positions the rotor within the induction geometry. When externally driven, the rotor can induce voltage in appropriately arranged coils.

OPERATING MODE B

Rotor Disengaged

Vertical repositioning changes magnetic coupling. A separate mechanism may be required for reliable braking or full mechanical stopping.

06 / Electrical Architecture

The three-supercapacitor system

My original design proposed three energy-storage elements with coordinated roles.

C1 / PRIMARY

Charging Bank

Receives rectified generator energy through appropriate charge regulation.

C2 / BUFFER

Load Supply

Supports the regulated output while sufficient usable energy remains.

C3 / RESERVE

Reserve Management

Provides additional capacity or accepts surplus energy within electrical safety limits.

Proposed energy path

Mechanical Input Measured external energy
→
Rotor Generator Induced voltage
→
Rectifier + Control Charge regulation
→
C1 / C2 / C3 Managed energy storage
→
Regulated Load Measured DC output

Capacitor energy calculation

E = ½ × C × V²

E is stored energy in joules, C is capacitance in farads, and V is voltage.

A 100-farad capacitor charged to 2.7 volts stores approximately 364.5 joules.

Usable energy is lower when minimum operating voltage, leakage, conversion losses, and control requirements are taken into account.

Critical design principle

Supercapacitors store energy; switching between them cannot create additional energy. The system must still receive sufficient input energy to support the electrical load and losses.

09 / The Bigger Vision

What if power generation became part of the device itself?

Imagine electronics designed not only to consume electricity, but also to recover, regulate, and reuse available energy.

My original QIBS idea was not limited to constructing a miniature laboratory generator.

The long-term ambition is to explore whether compact induction-based energy modules could be integrated into everyday electronics or other systems with access to recoverable mechanical energy.

A suitable source might include mechanical motion, vibration, rotation, or another externally supplied drive mechanism.

If enough usable energy can be recovered relative to consumption, a product might need less frequent charging—or, in a suitable low-power application, potentially operate without routine external charging.

The imagined finished technology

The central engineering question

Where does the continuing mechanical energy come from? A generator embedded in an otherwise stationary device cannot sustain its own output merely through magnets, capacitors, or electrical feedback.

10 / Modular Power Architecture

From one QIBS module to an integrated array

Another part of the concept is scalability: multiple miniature modules arranged on an electronic board alongside ordinary circuitry.

A proposed motherboard-scale system could contain several QIBS modules, individual generator rectifiers, controlled DC combining, supercapacitor storage, regulation, and monitoring.

The purpose of multiple modules would be to investigate whether their separately supplied mechanical input powers can be converted into a larger combined electrical output.

ARRAY / 01

ARRAY / 02

Two Modules

ARRAY / 04

Four Modules

Investigate scaling, thermal behavior, electrical regulation, and synchronization requirements.

The scaling question

Pout,total = Σ Pout,module − Pcombining losses

This simplified expression describes the combined electrical output after shared conversion losses. It does not mean adding modules creates extra energy.

Every module still requires mechanical energy. A larger array may produce more total output only when its increased input energy and electrical design support that result.

Miniaturization challenge

The smaller the generator, the harder it may become to fit useful magnetic structures, bearings, actuators, and energy storage into the space. The dimensions and power values shown in concept images are visual proposals, not achievable targets established by testing.

11 / Future Application Concepts

Could everyday electronics need less charging?

This is the imaginative part of the QIBS research project.

What if some products could harvest useful mechanical energy during normal use and manage it through integrated storage and power electronics?

APPLICATION / 01

Low-Power Sensors

Devices located near vibration or moving machinery could investigate energy recovery for sensing and intermittent wireless communication.

APPLICATION / 02

Portable Electronics

Explore whether sufficiently available motion energy could supplement ordinary rechargeable power systems.

APPLICATION / 03

Appliance Systems

Study energy recovery in equipment with existing mechanical motion, while measuring the added drag and net energy impact.

The goal isn't to promise impossible energy. It's to imagine better ways to capture and use energy that would otherwise be wasted.

The hypothesis worth investigating

A useful device-level experiment would compare the electrical energy a QIBS module actually delivers against the energy the complete device consumes.

It would also measure the mechanical energy required to operate the generator, including additional drag imposed on the source.

If harvested power meets or exceeds average load demand under sustained operating conditions, the device may maintain its stored energy without external charging for that period.

If harvested power is lower than consumption, the device still needs another energy source. In that case, energy harvesting might extend runtime rather than eliminate charging.

12 / Scientific Validation

The test that matters most: energy balance

A spinning rotor alone is not proof of electrical self-sufficiency.

We must measure the energy entering and leaving the complete system.

ΔEstored = Einput − Eoutput − Elosses

The experimental plan includes:

  • Reconstruct and measure rotor and magnet geometry.
  • Measure bearing friction and coast-down behavior.
  • Measure mechanical drive torque and rotational speed.
  • Measure induced coil voltage and current under load.
  • Measure rectifier and regulator conversion efficiency.
  • Record individual capacitor voltage and energy changes.
  • Record vertical actuator energy consumption.
  • Measure output power and operating duration.
  • Compare single-module and multi-module performance at comparable conditions.
  • Test representative loads for net energy gain from externally supplied motion.
What success would actually mean

Success could mean a more compact generator, efficient energy recovery, longer operating time, useful integrated storage, or a practical niche application. It does not require demonstrating perpetual motion or energy creation.
